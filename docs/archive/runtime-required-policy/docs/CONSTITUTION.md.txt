# Project Harness Constitution

1. **Customer intent remains attributable.** Preserve raw requests and supplied artifacts; record interpretation and derived requirements separately.
2. **Waterfall stages provide detail; agile revision keeps them current.** Discovery, requirements, design, planning, implementation, verification, acceptance, deployment, closure, and maintenance have explicit inputs, deliverables, dependencies, and exit criteria. Inputs may arrive in any order and a completed stage may be revisited. Iterations establish the necessary upstream basis for their scope before dependent implementation; every implementation change has a TASK in a currently approved PLAN. Apply the stage and change rules in [Development method](DEVELOPMENT_METHOD.md).
3. **Uncertainty stays visible and fails closed.** Unknowns, conflicts, assumptions, and ambiguous evidence remain active open items until an authorized resolution is propagated and evidenced.
4. **One informed decision authorizes the described change.** The agent assesses feasibility and impact, prepares the concrete work and verification scope, and asks once whether to proceed. The owner's explicit prompt response to that proposal is the change approval and is recorded as the required PLAN decision without a second approval ceremony. One decision can cover several plans and tasks within the agreed scope. The agent records the decision; the runtime checks records and execution conditions, never deciding for the owner. No user-run command or typed fingerprint is required for routine approval. A bare request or document does not provide that decision. Current runtime gates and the cryptographic boundaries for production, production data, destructive operations, secret access and final customer acceptance remain in force; agents never self-grant a decision.
5. **Progress is evidence-based.** Code existence is not verification, verification is not customer acceptance, and deployment is not closure.
6. **Prior approval survives as history for its version.** Requirements, plans and deliverables may change throughout the project. Preserve the prior approved baseline and its decisions and evidence; a revision does not retroactively make completed work unapproved. A change to agreed requirements, acceptance criteria, scope or material constraints needs a change record and impact analysis. The new version needs the applicable change decision and verification of changed and affected parts. Retain unaffected evidence only while applicable, and expose later defects or contradictory findings honestly.
7. **Collaboration preserves ownership.** A single writer normally works directly. A requested formal result/handoff or legacy mode may require a prospective claim under the compatibility procedure in [Collaboration](COLLABORATION.md#formal-records-for-a-single-writer); it never establishes retrospective ownership. Concurrent writers use exact handoffs or isolated worktrees with non-overlapping path and logical-resource claims.
8. **Stop work according to impact and actual authority.** Missing inputs or unresolved changes block dependent work; unrelated work may continue under current authority. Explicit scope coverage checks the affected task and its subjects; shared dependency changes or global blockers can still stop broader work. Legacy exact-bound plans retain their broader currentness checks. Neither case revokes prior-version approval history; never bypass the gate.
9. **Recorded attempts and failures are retained.** When an audited result workflow is used, the latest attempt controls it. Failed, incomplete, stale, superseded, and rejected records remain history.
10. **Names are not identities.** Agent and reviewer IDs provide procedural provenance; hostile separation and protected execution belong to an external orchestrator or CI boundary.
11. **Derived views are disposable.** Human reports and `HANDOFF.md` may be regenerated; canonical records, shared runtime state, Git history, and protected authority records keep their distinct roles.
12. **Responsible uncertainty beats fabricated certainty.** When a completion claim cannot be proven at the required verification profile, the correct state is unresolved.
13. **Two inputs carry a project.** What the customer supplied and what the developer says to the agent are the only inputs a project needs from intake to closure; the agent derives every record, gate, and report from them. The owner's decision is one of those prompts. The only step outside them is the signature on a high-risk request, and that is by design.
14. **Planning completion and execution readiness are distinct.** A planning-only request ends with the authored plan, passing required planning checks and reported open items; it does not require an execution approval request. Execution readiness requires the current owner decision, its application and a passing TASK gate. Stage reviews add no separate sign-off unless the explicit contract requires it. Planning completion, an iteration review or a demonstration grants no execution authority or customer acceptance. See [Development method](DEVELOPMENT_METHOD.md#planning-complete-and-execution-ready).
15. **Plans organize work and remain adjustable.** Requirements give rise to tasks whose detail and order may be determined during planning or adjusted to user priorities. The agent may add, split and reorder work within the agreed outcomes and constraints, respecting technical prerequisites and explaining material tradeoffs. Routine refinement is distinct from changing the agreement. Versioned execution-scope coverage supports this refinement and sharing across single-level plans; legacy exact-bound approvals retain their original semantics.
16. **Reconfirm material deltas, not bookkeeping.** Ask again when the proposed outcome, impact, important assumptions, conditions or authority materially change beyond the user's decision. Pause affected work and present that delta. Task splitting, ordering or record creation within the described change do not themselves justify repeating the same approval. Legacy compatibility limits and high-risk requirements must be named separately.
